Dear Maestros, Intendants, and Artistic Administrators,

We love you. We really do. We know how much you juggle—budgets, schedules, egos, crises. We know you're doing your best under impossible constraints. But from our place under the lights, there are some things we <a href="about.html">deeply wish you understood</a>. Not to accuse, not to criticize—but to help us all make better art, together.

Because if you listen carefully, the voice onstage might be telling you more than just the score.

## 1. Our Instrument Is Our Body—And It's Always in Flux

Unlike a violinist or flutist, we can't replace strings or polish valves. Singers carry our instruments in our ribs, throat, lungs, and nervous system. That means we walk into rehearsal with the same body that slept poorly, fought a cold, or held its breath after a tense phone call.

We train for years—decades—to master breath, diction, stamina, and stage presence. But no amount of training can undo vocal damage from overwork, poor scheduling, or unsafe rehearsal environments. Late-night rehearsals before a double show day? Sudden outdoor performances in 4°C weather with no warmup? These choices have real consequences and can lead to <a href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6206709/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">long-term vocal injury</a>.

That doesn't mean we can't sing. We're trained for this. But it does mean nuance. Some days we're warming up slower. Some days we're protecting a note so we can give you everything tomorrow. When you understand that, we feel safer—and we sing better.

## 2. Your Energy Sets the Emotional Tone—And We Feel Everything

If you're angry, sarcastic, cold, or rushed, we feel it in our bones. We're trained to read emotional subtext—it's literally our job. That hyper-sensitivity doesn't turn off when rehearsal ends. If you want us to give emotionally authentic performances, you have to create a space where vulnerability feels safe. The <a href="https://www.helpmusicians.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Help Musicians</a> charity has documented how crucial emotional safety is for artistic performance.

We know you're tired. We are too. But compassionate leadership isn't weakness—it's precision. A smile. A proper introduction. Saying thank you after a hard day. These things cost nothing and change everything.

## 3. Honest, Constructive Feedback Builds Trust. Vague Comments Break It

We don't need to be in charge—but we do need to feel seen. Singers are not delicate. We live on feedback. We know when a phrase isn't working and crave the insight that helps us fix it. But vague comments like "make it more interesting" or "it just doesn't move me," without actionable input, do nothing but confuse and demoralize.

Some of us have studied this <a href="repertoire.html">repertoire for years</a>. Some of us live inside these characters more deeply than you realize. When we bring up musical phrasing, vocal comfort, or staging concerns, we're not being "difficult." We're trying to protect the integrity of the performance. Trust our artistry, and you'll get more inspired performances—and far fewer passive-aggressive emails.

And for the love of music, respond to auditions. There are companies I've auditioned for years ago who never sent a yes or a no. Not even a form letter. It's demoralizing, and it reflects poorly on the company.

## 4. Not Every Good Singer Shouts. Not Every Big Voice is Loud

Conductors sometimes want the "Wagner sound" from every singer, in every role. But real vocal color is more complex. A huge voice might be warm and intimate. A lyric voice might carry effortlessly over the orchestra. Dynamics are choices, not limitations.

If a singer is giving you detail, risk, and phrasing, don't ask for more volume until you've really listened. Bigger is not always better. And bark is not always power.

## 5. Time and Space for Warm-Up Is Not Negotiable

Time on stage is not the same as time in the room. A five-hour rehearsal where we sit for four and sing for one is not "only one hour of work." The physical, emotional, and psychological load of waiting, staying warm, and staying focused is immense.

Too often, singers arrive at a theater only to find there's no warm-up room, or that it's adjacent to the stage and unusable during rehearsal. Warming up in a bathroom stall is humiliating. And warming up cold in front of an audience is unsafe. The <a href="https://www.voicefoundation.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Voice Foundation</a> emphasizes that proper vocal warm-up is essential for injury prevention.

Protecting our instrument starts with protecting our warm-up. Build it into the day. Prioritize it like you would tuning the orchestra.

## 6. We Don't Want to Say No. We Want to Say Yes—Safely

When a singer hesitates or resists a request, 90% of the time it's not ego—it's survival. We want the high note, the leap off the scaffold, the extra rehearsal with the maestro. But we also want to sing again tomorrow. And the day after.

Trust us when we say, "That's not safe today." We're not lazy. We're protecting the music.

## 7. Rehearsals Must Be Safe Spaces. Without That, You Don't Get Art—You Get Caution

Rehearsals are not just logistics—they are laboratories. A space to try, fail, rework, and find something honest. But when kindness and respect are missing, that space collapses into fear. And when artists are afraid, they stop creating. They start protecting.

Singers carry enormous pressure into rehearsal rooms: we're being paid (often not enough), observed, and judged—sometimes all at once. Especially in countries outside the German-speaking world, where freelance singers rarely enjoy stable contracts or social protections, every rehearsal feels like part of an audition for the next gig. Organizations like <a href="https://www.operaamerica.org/">Opera America</a> and the <a href="https://www.agma.org/">American Guild of Musical Artists</a> work to address these industry challenges, while <a href="https://www.equity.org.uk/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Equity</a> advocates for fair working conditions in the UK, but much depends on individual theater cultures.

If trying something new becomes a professional risk, no one tries.

It has to be okay to experiment and fail. Sometimes a bold vocal color won't land. Sometimes a physical choice looks wrong before it feels right. That's the work. But if we're mocked, shut down, or shamed the moment something "doesn't work," the message is clear: stay safe. And safe singing rarely moves an audience.

Kindness is not sentimentality. It's a condition for excellence. The best conductors and directors I've worked with lead through generosity. They create rooms where singers feel seen, trusted, and free to dig deep. For more insights on creating supportive environments for <a href="https://www.nats.org/">vocal health and artistry</a>, resources from the National Association of Teachers of Singing offer valuable guidance, as does <a href="https://www.classicalsinger.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Classical Singer Magazine</a> with its focus on professional development.

That's how you get magic.

## The Art We Make Together Is Sacred

We chose this work because it moves us. Because it cracks open hearts. Because it builds bridges across language, culture, and time. We want to give everything we've got—but we can only do that when we're treated as full humans, not just voice-delivery systems.

The way you treat singers matters. It shapes the art. It echoes in the sound. And the best part? It costs nothing.

So here's our ask: See us. Hear us. Trust us. Lead with kindness. Communicate clearly. Create space for risk and growth. If you're interested in exploring how we can <a href="reach-me.html">work together</a> to create exceptional art, let's start the conversation.

And we'll meet you on stage—whole, ready, and singing from the soul.

With respect and solidarity,

A singer who wants to give you their best
