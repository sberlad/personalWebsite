A costume can change more quickly than a body believes it has changed.

That is the problem I want to solve in preparing the Korporal and Graf von Sandau in *Das Wirtshaus im Spessart*. <a href="https://harztheater.de/produktion/das-wirtshaus-im-spessart" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Harztheater lists both roles together under my name</a>. On a cast list, the slash is economical. Onstage, I have to decide what happens on either side of it.

### Two systems of authority

I begin with behavior rather than disguise. How does each man assume he will be heard? What does he do when somebody fails to listen? Does he occupy space because he is accustomed to giving orders, or because his social position seems to guarantee that space?

These questions allow the characters to differ without requiring two unrelated vocal caricatures. I can change the direction of a sentence, the timing of a response or the amount of permission I seem to ask before moving. The audience reads those choices very quickly.

I want the distinction to remain clear even in ordinary moments. If a character exists only in his most exaggerated gesture, there is little left when the scene requires him simply to listen.

### The change begins before the entrance

Quick-change work needs a practical sequence. Costume and props must be ready, but so must the next intention. What has this man just been doing? What does he expect to find when he enters? If I arrive thinking only about whether every fastening is closed, the change is technically complete and dramatically unfinished.

My preparation therefore needs a small number of reliable cues: a posture I can inhabit without rigidity, a pace that belongs to the character, a clear first objective. Too many details become another costume I have to put on.

The breath is useful here because it connects the practical and dramatic tasks. I need enough freedom to sing, and enough attention to stop carrying the previous man's urgency into the next scene.

### A shared instrument does not need disguising

The pleasure of doubling is that an audience may recognize the performer and still accept the distinction. Theatre does not always require us to erase its machinery. It requires us to make the agreement convincing.

I have sung contrasting parts within a single evening before, including Uberto and Schlendrian. Returning to that craft in a different idiom reminds me that versatility is often a matter of precision rather than transformation on a grand scale.

I do not need to become unrecognizable. I need to make each man's next action feel inevitable to him. The clothes help the audience see who has arrived. The behavior gives them a reason to believe it.
