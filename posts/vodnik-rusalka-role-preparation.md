Excerpt: Singing Vodník in German, Samuel Berlad explores the father-daughter bond in Dvořák’s Rusalka and the dramatic work of remaining silently present onstage.
---

I sang Vodník in German. The most interesting language I had to find, though, was that of a father who cannot protect his daughter from the life she wants.

That sounds like a clear description of a role. In rehearsal and performance, it became a much less comfortable question: when does protection become an inability to let her go?

### Finding the father inside the water sprite

In <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton%C3%ADn_Dvo%C5%99%C3%A1k" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Dvořák</a>’s *Rusalka*, Vodník can easily become a supernatural authority who understands the danger before everybody else. <a href="https://www.metopera.org/learn/education/learning-library/educator-guide-pages/rusalka/plot-and-creation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The opera’s story gives him ample reason to fear his daughter’s passage into the human world</a>. But knowing that danger exists is not the same as knowing what your daughter needs from you.

I found the father figure more compelling than the figure who knows. He can warn her. He can grieve. He cannot make her desire disappear, and he cannot offer her the life she imagines elsewhere.

If I sing only his foreknowledge, the relationship has already ended before the scene begins. I want to leave room for him to listen, to hope that something he says might still reach her, and to discover that love does not give him the power he needs.

### Remaining when the score gives me nothing to sing

That paternal relationship became so central to our production that I was onstage much more than the score calls for. I was present during Rusalka’s encounter with Ježibaba near the beginning, and for Rusalka’s death at the end as we staged it.

Those are choices from our production, not a description of the opera’s standard ending. Their importance for me was the continuity they gave the father’s experience. He did not disappear from the daughter’s story simply because he had stopped singing.

A silent presence changes the work. With no phrase to carry the thought, I have to know what I am watching and how it alters me. I cannot comment visibly on every event. Nor can I become a piece of scenery waiting for my next entry.

Being there also makes helplessness concrete. The father occupies the same space as what is happening and still cannot prevent it. His presence offers no guarantee of protection.

### Grief without a continuous dark color

Singing the role in German gave the words an immediacy I could work with directly. I wanted to use that immediacy to distinguish his different attempts to reach her. A warning, an appeal and an expression of grief cannot all be the same solemn phrase.

The bass voice supplies authority readily. What interested me was allowing that authority to fail without making the voice itself collapse. The line still needs direction; the person singing it may no longer know what to do.

Vodník’s grief became most moving to me when I stopped treating it as the defining atmosphere of the role. It grew out of the relationship: a father staying, watching, trying to remain available, long after his certainty had ceased to be useful.
