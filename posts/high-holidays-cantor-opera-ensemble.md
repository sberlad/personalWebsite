By December, the High Holidays are behind us. That makes it a useful time to ask what their preparation required, before the calendar begins its next round of promises.

For a singer whose professional life connects a German theatre with Jewish communities beyond it, the distance is not only geographical. A rehearsal schedule and a liturgical calendar organize responsibility in different ways. Both expect you to be present.

### Two calendars, one instrument

Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur bring a particular musical and spiritual concentration. <a href="https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/the-high-holidays/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">My Jewish Learning describes how the machzor's texts and the service's musical language carry the themes of the High Holidays</a>. For the cantor, that concentration requires preparation long before the first public phrase.

The theatre's season has its own demands: rehearsals, performances and colleagues whose work depends on shared availability. A meaningful cantorial commitment cannot become something I fit into the spaces left over after the theatre has finished with me.

I think of the practical work as part of the musical work. Dates need to be discussed early. Travel needs room around it. Repertoire needs to be ready before the final rush. A plan that works only if every connection runs perfectly is asking the voice to absorb the consequences of optimism.

### Familiar prayer still needs preparation

Knowing the words is different from being ready to lead them. I need to consider the community's customs, the expected flow of the service and where people will join in. What I can sing is only one part of what I should choose.

This becomes especially clear when moving between communities. Familiarity with a prayer does not guarantee familiarity with the local way of praying it. Listening before leading is a form of respect.

There is also the problem of artistic appetite. Opera encourages me to think about the expressive possibility of a passage. Chazzanut asks an additional question: will that choice help this congregation pray at this moment?

### Returning without dividing myself

I do not become a different person when I return to the theatre. The attention to words, the responsibility for pace and the experience of giving space to other voices all come with me.

But I want to resist turning that connection into an easy slogan about two professions enriching each other. They do enrich each other; they also compete for time, energy and preparation. Acknowledging that competition makes the connection more honest.

One voice cannot be in two places at once. It can arrive fully in one place, then fully in the next—if I have done the less visible work that makes presence possible.
