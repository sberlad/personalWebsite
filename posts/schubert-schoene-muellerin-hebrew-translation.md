---

The easiest objection to singing <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Schubert" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Schubert</a> in Hebrew is also the strongest: the music was written for German words.

I agree. What surprised me in Ido Ariel’s translation was how much of those words’ musical life could survive in another language.

In 2024, with Ido at the piano, I sang the Hebrew premiere of his translation of the complete *Die schöne Müllerin*. I had expected to negotiate the distance between the languages. I had not expected the distance to feel so small in so many places.

### Translating the movement of the words

<a href="https://www.digital.wienbibliothek.at/wbrobv/content/titleinfo/436250" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Schubert’s cycle sets Wilhelm Müller’s poems; the Wienbibliothek preserves an 1823 manuscript score</a>. German is built into the music through more than meaning. There is the placement of a stress, the resistance of a consonant, the point at which a word releases into a vowel. Those things help a phrase move.

Ido mirrored the rhythmic and consonantal structure of the German exceptionally well. The Hebrew did not merely fit the available notes. Again and again, it preserved something of the physical argument of the original: where the language gathers energy, where it interrupts itself, where it lets the musical line continue.

That changes the singer’s task. Instead of constantly compensating for a new text, I could recognize musical impulses I already knew and find them working through Hebrew. The translation respected the mouth as well as the dictionary.

### Even the idioms

The idiomatic accuracy impressed me just as much. An idiom is difficult because translating its words may leave its meaning behind, while explaining its meaning may destroy its concision. In a song, there is also a rhythm waiting for the answer.

In some cases, I helped Ido sharpen the meaning of the German idioms. That was part of our collaboration: looking closely at what a phrase actually does, beyond what its individual words appear to say. His ability to bring those meanings into singable Hebrew was remarkable.

I do not mean that the two languages became identical. I mean that their differences had been worked through with unusual care. That gave me room to interpret, rather than spend the performance apologizing musically for the translation.

### A new vernacular repertoire, with old roots

I also hear this project within the tradition of bringing folk songs and art songs into Hebrew as part of the modern language revival. Early Zionist culture needed a growing repertoire for everyday singing, education and shared secular life. <a href="https://www.iemj.org/en/les-musiques-en-israel/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Translation and adaptation helped build it alongside new composition; the European Institute of Jewish Music traces the borrowing of Slavic and Yiddish melodies in that development</a>.

This was no musical beginning from nothing. Hebrew already had centuries of poetry, prayer and song, and Jewish communities carried rich, varied musical traditions. What was being built was a modern vernacular song culture for new circumstances. Translating existing repertoire was one way of making that culture inhabitable.

Schubert in Hebrew belongs, for me, to that continuing impulse. A language can make room for music that came from elsewhere without pretending it has no history of its own.

The gain is intimacy: the listener can encounter the miller’s hopes and self-deceptions directly in Hebrew. With Ido’s translation, that proximity did not demand nearly as much distance from the German as I had feared. Fidelity became something I could feel in the act of singing.
