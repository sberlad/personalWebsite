When people discover that I'm both a <a href="about.html">chazzan (cantor)</a> and an <a href="repertoire.html">opera singer</a>, their first question is often, "Do those two worlds ever get mixed up?" The answer is yes—but not in the way they expect. The disciplines of <strong>chazzanut</strong> and <strong>classical singing</strong> don't merely coexist for me. They are in constant, vibrant conversation.

## The Voice as Vessel of Soul

One of the most formative ideas I encountered as a voice student came from <a href="https://thomasheyer.com/">Prof. Thomas Heyer</a>, my second voice teacher during university. He loved to (intentionally) mis-translate the word <em>person</em> from Latin <em>per sonare</em>: "to sound through." While not linguistically accurate, the idea behind it was profound. To him, and eventually to me, the <strong>voice is the vessel of the soul</strong>. It doesn't just express emotion—it carries the singer's inner being into the world.

In both <strong>operatic performance</strong> and <strong>cantorial prayer</strong>, this principle holds true. On the stage, the voice tells a character's story. On the bimah (the platform in a synagogue from which the Torah is read), it carries the spiritual weight of an entire congregation. But in both, if the sound doesn't come from something authentic and grounded, it fails to move anyone.

## Breathing as Spiritual Connection

### The Physiology of Empathy

Another of Heyer's teachings continues to shape how I sing and how I lead prayer. He believed that proper breath support doesn't just produce better tone; it creates <strong>physiological empathy</strong>. If a singer breathes fully and openly, the audience unconsciously mirrors that breath. They <strong>experience the emotion</strong> with you, literally breathing it in.

That shared breath is everything. In <a href="https://www.cantors.org/">cantorial music</a>, this is especially important. A chazzan doesn't perform to impress; he sings to elevate. The congregation's prayers are carried upward on his breath. And in that moment, voice becomes <strong>spiritual connection</strong>.

### Breath as Prayer

This concept becomes particularly powerful during the <a href="https://www.myjewishlearning.com/article/unetaneh-tokef/">Unetaneh Tokef</a> on Yom Kippur, one of the most emotionally intense moments in the Jewish liturgical year. The cantor's breath literally carries the congregation's hopes and fears. Similarly, when singing Rodrigo di Posa's death scene in Verdi's Don Carlo or Mimi's final moments in La Bohème, the breath becomes the character's life force ebbing away. The technique is the same; the spiritual weight differs.

## Technical Foundations Across Traditions

People often ask if operatic training interferes with <strong>Jewish liturgical singing</strong>. The short answer: not if you know what you're doing. Both require clarity of diction, legato line, color, and intention. The emotional landscape might differ, but the <strong>technical foundation</strong> is the same.

### Shared Vocal Principles

Both traditions demand:

<strong>Textual Clarity</strong>: Whether singing Mozart's Italian or Hebrew prayers, every word must be understood. <strong>Dynamic Control</strong>: From the softest <em>pianissimo</em> in a Schubert lied to the hushed intensity of <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3LB_mZj4FNM">Kol Nidre</a>, as demonstrated by my late teacher and mentor, the great Cantor Naftali Herstik. <strong>Emotional Honesty</strong>: Audiences and congregations can sense when something is artificial.

Opera taught me how to use <strong>stillness, timing, phrasing</strong>, and even silence to shape narrative. Chazzanut taught me how to mean every word. I carry both of those lessons whether I'm singing Mozart or leading Yom Kippur <strong>Unetaneh Tokef</strong>.

### The Art of Sacred Performance

What differs is the intention. In opera, I serve the composer's vision and the director's concept. In chazzanut, I serve something higher—facilitating the congregation's connection to the divine. Yet both require the same vulnerability, the same willingness to be a vessel for something larger than oneself.

This dual practice has made me a better artist in both spheres. My <a href="repertoire.html">operatic performances</a> carry deeper spiritual weight, while my cantorial work benefits from the dramatic training and technical precision that opera demands.

## Two Callings, One Voice

This isn't a juggling act. I don't compartmentalize my artistic and spiritual selves. Whether I'm standing on the <strong>operatic stage</strong> or on the <strong>bimah</strong>, I bring the same breath, the same technique, the same open-hearted intent. These aren't two paths. They are one voice, sounding through.

For other singers exploring the intersection of sacred and secular music, I encourage you to embrace both traditions fully. Don't water down either for the sake of the other. Let them inform and strengthen each other, as they have for me.

The synagogue and the opera house are both spaces where music transcends the ordinary. Understanding this connection has made me not just a more complete artist, but a more complete human being.
