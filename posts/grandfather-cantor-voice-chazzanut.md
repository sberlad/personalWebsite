Excerpt: Samuel Berlad reflects on his grandfather, Miami cantor Jack Lerner, and an Ahavas Olam setting passed down by his mother: inheritance, memory and authorship.
---

My mother taught me a setting of Ahavas Olam that my grandfather, Jack Lerner, used to sing. He was a cantor at B’nai Raphael in Miami. I have not found that setting anywhere else so far.

I suspect he wrote it. I cannot say that he did.

That small uncertainty has become part of what the music means to me. I know how it reached me. I do not know exactly where it began.

### A melody with a family history

When we talk about inherited repertoire, we can make the process sound very orderly: a composer writes a piece, a singer performs it, the next generation learns it. Here, the path I can name is more intimate. My grandfather sang it. My mother taught it to me. Now I carry it.

The absence of another version is suggestive, but it is not proof of authorship. He may have composed it. He may have learned it from somebody whose name did not travel with the melody. I want to leave that question open without making the connection feel any less real.

Calling it “my grandfather’s Ahavas Olam” describes a relationship I can stand behind. Calling it “Ahavas Olam, composed by Jack Lerner” would claim something I have yet to establish.

### What actually survives?

<a href="https://www.milkenarchive.org/articles/view/introduction-to-volume-14/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The Milken Archive’s introduction to American cantorial art</a> describes a musical tradition with a life both within worship and on the concert platform. My own connection to that inheritance begins on a smaller scale: one setting, passed through my mother.

That matters because a family can preserve something without preserving its documentation. A melody remains singable while the circumstances of its creation become uncertain. The music reaches the next generation more completely than the explanation does.

I find something moving, and slightly unsettling, in that imbalance. The voice can carry what the record cannot confirm.

### Inheritance still asks something of the singer

Knowing that a setting belongs to my family’s musical life does not settle how I should sing it. My instrument has its own proportions. The congregation in front of me has its own relationship to the prayer. Affection cannot make those differences disappear.

[In my earlier reflection on chazzanut and opera](blog.html#post-from-shul-to-stage), I wrote about bringing one voice to both callings. This melody gives that idea a particular responsibility. I want to honor the way it reached me while allowing it to function as prayer now.

There is a temptation to resolve the story beautifully: my grandfather wrote it, my mother preserved it, and I continue it. Perhaps that is what happened. Until I know, I prefer the story I can tell honestly.

My mother taught me her father’s Ahavas Olam. I can sing it. Some of its history is still waiting to be found.
