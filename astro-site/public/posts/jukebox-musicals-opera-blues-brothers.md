---

Classical training is useful until I start using it as proof that I know how everything should sound.

That is the uncomfortable part of moving into a show such as *Blues Brothers*. I bring years of work on breath, resonance, language and stamina. Then the music asks a very simple question: can you actually sit inside this rhythm?

An impressive answer in the wrong idiom is still the wrong answer.

### What travels with the voice

As Curtis in *Blues Brothers* at Harztheater, I also took on James Brown and Ray Charles. That placed me in a different musical world from Wagner or Schubert. Those names carry musical histories that an operatic sound cannot simply absorb by becoming louder or more enthusiastic.

What transfers is the capacity to prepare. I can break down a phrase, recognize where I am spending breath, hear when a vowel is interfering with a pitch, and repeat something without treating repetition as punishment. Listening to the ensemble remains essential. So does giving the text a reason to exist.

But the hierarchy changes. In opera, I may organize a passage around the travel of a sustained line. Here, the placement of a word against the beat can determine whether the whole phrase feels alive. I have to hear the groove as structure, rather than accompaniment underneath my singing.

### What needs to stay behind

One conservatory reflex is to finish every sound beautifully. Another is to assume that carrying a voice across a room is always the central task. Amplification changes that relationship. The microphone becomes part of the sound, and I need to hear what it is already doing before adding more.

That does not mean a classical singer should manufacture roughness to demonstrate authenticity. An imitation of somebody else's vocal history can be as mannered as an operatic phrase in the wrong place. I would rather find a credible rhythmic and verbal approach in my own instrument than borrow an effect I cannot sustain.

There is humility in admitting that support and stylistic fluency are different achievements.

### The lesson that comes back to opera

The surprise is how useful this becomes when I return to classical repertoire. Am I shaping the phrase because the character needs it, or because that is what a trained singer does at the end of a phrase? Am I listening to the orchestra, or expecting it to follow the sound I have already decided to make?

Curtis reminds me that technique earns its place through service. It gives me choices. The music still gets to decide which choices belong.
