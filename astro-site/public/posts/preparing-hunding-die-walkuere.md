---

Hunding is often played as a brute: Fricka’s bloodhound, a threatening body sent to enforce a moral order somebody else has defined.

I understand why. He is dangerous, and the world of his marriage is hardly an inviting one. But that image did not give me the most interesting way into him. I found an elegant, educated man who is true to his word. In the ordinary sense of those terms, I came to see him as the only honest and principled character in the piece.

That does not make him good. It makes the piece harder to dismiss.

### He means what he says

<a href="https://www.opera.lv/en/production/die-walkre/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">In the story of *Die Walküre*</a>, Siegmund seeks shelter in Hunding’s house. Hunding discovers that the stranger is his enemy. He nevertheless grants him hospitality for the night and tells him plainly that they will fight the next day.

There is no hidden bargain in that statement. Hunding does not pretend to offer friendship while arranging something else. He observes a code even when doing so gives shelter to a man he intends to kill.

Around him, identities are concealed, commitments collide, and exceptional desires demand exceptions to the rules. Hunding’s position is comparatively straightforward. You may find his principles appalling, but you do not have to guess what they are.

### Elegance makes a different kind of menace

If I begin by making him coarse, I tell the audience very quickly how to judge him. His brutality becomes a personal deficiency. An elegant, educated Hunding asks a more difficult question: what if the violence belongs comfortably inside the respectable order?

That affected how I wanted to use the voice. I did not need a growl to establish danger. Clear language, a composed bearing and a phrase that knows where it is going can suggest somebody accustomed to having his statements accepted.

Education need not produce kindness. Good manners need not imply a generous moral imagination. I wanted those qualities to coexist without treating the refinement as a disguise that would eventually fall away to reveal the “real” brute underneath. The refinement is part of the man too.

### Principled according to whose principles?

The qualification matters. I mean honest and principled in the ordinary, socially recognizable sense: he says what he means, keeps his word and acts according to the obligations he accepts. That is my reading of him, not a request to approve of his treatment of Sieglinde or the society he represents.

Wagner places those ordinary virtues under extraordinary pressure. The lovers’ claims move us, yet their urgency does not magically make Hunding deceitful. His consistency exposes a gap between living by a code and living justly.

Calling him Fricka’s bloodhound can obscure that independence. He has his own account of what is right before the divine dispute recruits him into a larger argument.

I find him more unsettling when I let him retain it. A brute offers the comfort of recognition. A courteous man who keeps his word and remains entirely convinced of his rights asks what our respectability is actually worth.
