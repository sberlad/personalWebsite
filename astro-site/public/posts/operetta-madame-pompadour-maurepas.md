Title: Operetta Is Harder Than It Looks

Excerpt: Preparing Maurepas in Leo Fall's Madame Pompadour reveals why operetta demands exact language, musical ease and the discipline to make hard work look light.
---

“Light repertoire” is an excellent phrase if the purpose is to make a singer underestimate the work.

There are evenings in which an audience is meant to feel the weight of what we are doing. Operetta often asks us to remove the evidence. The rhythm must feel buoyant, the dialogue immediate, the social world effortless. None of that happens because we have decided to relax.

Preparing Comte de Maurepas in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leo_Fall" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Leo Fall</a>'s *Madame Pompadour* brings me back to that distinction between ease and lack of effort.

### Status has to move

Maurepas belongs to a world of rank, intrigue and people trying to outmaneuver one another. For me, its comic possibilities begin with taking that social position seriously.

A man convinced of his importance can be very funny. He becomes less funny if I underline his foolishness before he has had a chance to act. I need to understand what he believes his title permits him to do, then let other people test the limits of that belief.

The scene moves through changes of advantage. If my performance stays at one level of pompous indignation, I lose those changes and eventually leave myself nowhere to go.

### Dialogue does not excuse musical imprecision

Spoken theatre and singing create different expectations of time. Moving between them requires the next musical entrance to feel motivated without losing its place. A scene can sound conversational and still depend on precise preparation.

I find that the small details matter disproportionately: the word carrying the turn of thought, the breath that prepares the entrance without announcing it, the end of a sentence that leaves room for a colleague's response. If I use operatic breadth everywhere, the exchange can become ceremonious precisely where it needs to be quick.

Then the music asks for line again. I cannot solve the dialogue by abandoning the singing, or solve the singing by turning every sentence into a prelude to my next phrase.

### The audience should not inherit our effort

Calling operetta difficult can become its own kind of vanity. We want recognition for the labor underneath the pleasure. But the audience has not come to certify how hard I worked.

The real discipline is to prepare thoroughly enough that I can respond lightly. The freedom must be available in the moment, not displayed as an achievement.

Perhaps “light” is a perfectly good description after all—provided we understand that it describes what arrives across the footlights. We are responsible for everything it takes to get it there.
