Excerpt: Samuel Berlad compares Squenz in Britten's A Midsummer Night's Dream with Wagner's Alberich: comic timing, vocal technique and the psychology of control.
---

There is something suspicious about being asked to make people laugh and frighten them with the same instrument. Surely one of those jobs requires a different voice?

Squenz in the 2022/23 season and Alberich in 2023/24 gave me two very different ways of testing that question. They belonged to successive seasons, but the contrast stayed with me. What changes most is what I want the other people onstage to do.

Squenz—Quince in the English original of <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Britten" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Britten</a>'s *A Midsummer Night's Dream*—wants his theatrical enterprise to succeed. Alberich wants a world that has humiliated him to submit. Both are trying to organize people who refuse to behave. One becomes funny. The other becomes dangerous.

### Taking the amateur seriously

Squenz is a carpenter attempting to make theatre. <a href="https://www.boosey.de/pages/opera/moredetails?musicid=5633" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Britten's publisher lists Quince as a bass</a>, but the vocal label tells us little about the particular dignity of this man. He has work to do. The play matters to him.

If I enter a scene thinking, “Now I will be amusing,” I have already stepped outside it. I start decorating the text, anticipating a laugh, telling the audience that I know better than the character. But his seriousness is precisely what gives the situation its comic scale. He thinks he is managing a production. We see how little he can manage.

The technical work follows that seriousness: clear words, exact cues, enough freedom in the breath to respond when another character interrupts the plan. Comedy becomes difficult when I try to guarantee its effect.

### Letting menace arrive

Alberich tempts the opposite mistake. Because I know where his story goes, I can make him threatening before anything has happened to him. Then the humiliation becomes an illustration of a villain we already recognize, rather than an event that changes a person.

[In my earlier account of preparing Alberich](blog.html#post-becoming-wagners-alberich), I wrote about power and restraint. The useful connection with Squenz is that both characters need room to fail. Alberich's attempt to gain affection must matter before his rejection of love can matter.

Vocally, I want Wagner's longer argument to travel through the phrase. I cannot manufacture that weight by making every consonant heavy or every vowel dark. With Squenz, the rhythm of a reaction may do the dramatic work; with Alberich, an idea can gather force across a much longer span.

### The same breath, a different claim

I don't want a comic technique and a dangerous technique fighting inside my throat. I want a dependable instrument and a different relationship to time, language and the people around me.

Squenz tries to keep the show together. Alberich tries to make the world pay. The distance between them is enormous. The uncomfortable discovery is how close their need for control can feel.
