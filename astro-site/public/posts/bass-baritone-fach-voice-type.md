“Are you really a bass or really a baritone?”

I understand the question. Bass-baritone can sound like a refusal to decide. In a profession organized around casting categories, a hyphen can look suspiciously like an escape route.

For me, it is a useful description of a voice whose work does not always fit neatly into either neighboring drawer. Useful is different from complete.

### More than the highest and lowest note

A range chart gives us a beginning, not a casting decision. <a href="https://www.interlochen.org/online/determining-your-voice-type" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Interlochen's explanation of voice types</a> distinguishes range from tessitura and timbre: where the music spends its time and what the instrument naturally sounds like matter alongside its outer limits.

I can sing a note and still find a whole role uncomfortable. Equally, a role's extreme notes may look unremarkable on paper while its sustained demands prove considerable. The orchestra, the language, the duration and the way a part repeatedly crosses the voice all change the experience.

The practical question is therefore more specific than “Does this belong to my Fach?” Can I sing this music with freedom, clarity and enough reserve to repeat it within the season?

### A label has a job to do

I do not want to dismiss the Fach system. Categories help singers, teachers and theatres discuss repertoire. They can keep ambition attached to a realistic understanding of an instrument. Problems arise when the category becomes more authoritative than the listening that produced it.

My repertoire has taken me through comic servants, fathers, antagonists, song recitals and cantorial work. Those settings ask different things of the same voice. The continuity is not that they all sound alike. It is that I have to recognize where the instrument functions honestly in each one.

Calling myself a bass-baritone does not make every low male role suitable. Nor does it explain my relationship to text, rhythm or character. A vocal category cannot do the singer's artistic thinking.

### Room to develop without becoming shapeless

There is a danger in celebrating versatility too enthusiastically. “I sing many things” can become a way of avoiding the harder question of what I sing well and sustainably. A wide career still requires choices and boundaries.

As a singer trained in vocal pedagogy, I want classification to stay open to evidence: how the voice behaves, what improves with work, and what remains unnecessarily costly. That is an ongoing conversation, especially as an instrument develops.

The hyphen suits me because it leaves room for that conversation. It identifies a neighborhood. I still have to learn which houses I can actually live in.
