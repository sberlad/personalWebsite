The trouble with beautiful singing is that it can make an audience very willing to forgive you.

They may forgive an unclear word, a sentimental gesture, even a thought you have failed to finish. The sound provides its own pleasure. In *Die Dreigroschenoper*, I have to ask whether that pleasure sometimes lets everybody off too easily.

Preparing Trauerweidenwalter makes that question practical. What am I doing to the scene when my first instinct is to make the voice attractive?

### An attractive surface can carry an ugly proposition

<a href="https://www.kwf.org/news/the-threepenny-opera-celebrates-its-75th-anniversary/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">The Kurt Weill Foundation describes the work's combination of political bite with the sounds of Berlin dance bands and cabaret</a>. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Brecht</a> and <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kurt_Weill" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Weill</a>, working with <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisabeth_Hauptmann" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Elisabeth Hauptmann</a>, made something whose musical appeal does not settle its moral argument.

That matters because “Brechtian” can become a shortcut for chilly performance, just as “cabaret” can become a shortcut for a knowing sneer. Either shortcut makes the piece easier than it is. If I signal that I am above the character, the audience can join me there. We become clever people observing somebody else's corruption.

I would rather allow a statement to be persuasive enough that its implications become uncomfortable. The contradiction needs to remain active.

### Unlearning the automatic phrase

Classical training gives me habits of connection: carry the vowel, organize the breath, let a musical line travel. I value those habits. Here I need to notice when they soften an edge the text requires.

A word may need to land before I have made its sound agreeable. A rhythmic figure may need its plainness. A sentence may become less truthful when I swell toward its emotional center as though I were completing a Romantic confession.

This does not make strain an artistic virtue. I am not trying to injure the instrument or pretend that a trained voice is untrained. I am trying to choose a sound rather than automatically produce the one my education most readily rewards.

### Whose comfort does the sound serve?

There is still room for beauty in Weill. Its presence can sharpen the problem: why am I enjoying this, and what am I agreeing to while I enjoy it?

That question comes back to the singer. Is a beautiful phrase revealing the character's charm, exposing a contradiction, or simply reassuring me that I can sing?

The death in the title is therefore an ambition for a habit, not for the voice. I want beautiful singing to lose its automatic authority. Once it becomes one choice among others, I can listen for what the scene actually needs.
